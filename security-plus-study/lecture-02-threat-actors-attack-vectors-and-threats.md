2. What Is a Threat Actor?

A threat actor is a person, group or organisation that intentionally carries out activities that could compromise the security of a computer system, network or information.

For example, someone may attempt to access a company's database without permission to obtain customer information.

In this scenario:

Threat actor: The unauthorised individual.

Target: The company's database.

Objective: Obtain confidential information.

Potential impact: Loss of confidentiality and possible financial damage.

3. Types of Threat Actors

Different threat actors have different motivations, resources and capabilities.

3.1 Cybercriminals

Individuals or organised groups motivated by financial gain.

Examples include fraud, stealing information and extortion.

3.2 Nation-State Actors

Groups supported by or associated with governments.

Possible motivations include intelligence gathering, espionage and strategic interests.

3.3 Insider Threats

People inside an organisation who misuse their legitimate access, deliberately or accidentally.

Example: An employee improperly shares confidential company information.

3.4 Hacktivists

Individuals or groups motivated by political, social or ideological causes.

Their activities may involve disrupting or exposing an organisation's systems or information.

3.5 Security Researchers

People who investigate weaknesses to improve security, ideally with proper authorisation.

Their work includes responsible vulnerability discovery and reporting.

Important distinction: Not everyone who studies hacking is a criminal. Authorisation, purpose and conduct matter.

5. What Is an Attack Vector?

An attack vector is the path or method through which a threat actor attempts to compromise a system.

Common attack vectors include:

Email: Deceptive emails may trick people into revealing information or opening unsafe attachments.

Websites: Fake or compromised websites may attempt to collect credentials or expose visitors to malicious content.

Removable media: Untrusted USB drives and other removable devices can introduce security risks.

Text messages: Fraudulent messages may impersonate banks, delivery companies or other trusted services.

Software vulnerabilities: Outdated software may contain weaknesses that attackers could exploit.

6. Common Cybersecurity Threats

6.1 Malware

Malware means malicious software. It is software designed to perform harmful or unauthorised activities.

Common types include:

Malware

Meaning

Virus

Attaches itself to files or programs and can spread when they run

Worm

Can spread between systems without requiring a user to run an infected host file

Trojan

Disguises itself as legitimate or useful software

Ransomware

Restricts access to data or systems and demands payment

Spyware

Secretly collects information about a user's activities

Rootkit

Conceals malicious activity or provides unauthorised privileged access

Example: A user downloads an application that appears legitimate. Unknown to the user, it contains malicious software.

This illustrates why software sources and permissions matter.

6.2 Phishing

Phishing is a social engineering technique in which an attacker impersonates a trusted person or organisation to trick someone into revealing information or taking an unsafe action.

Examples include:

Fake bank security alerts.

Emails pretending to come from IT support.

Messages claiming that an account will be suspended.

Fake password-reset notifications.

Security analysts look for unusual sender addresses, unexpected requests, suspicious links, urgency and requests for confidential information.

6.3 Social Engineering

Social engineering involves manipulating people into revealing information or performing actions that compromise security.

Common examples:

Pretexting: Creating a false story or identity to gain someone's trust.

Baiting: Offering something attractive to encourage an unsafe action.

Tailgating: Following an authorised person into a restricted physical area without proper permission.

Impersonation: Pretending to be someone trusted.

People are an important part of an organisation's security environment.

6.4 Denial-of-Service (DoS)

A Denial-of-Service attack attempts to make a system or service unavailable to legitimate users.

Imagine a website receiving so many unwanted requests that genuine visitors cannot access it.

A Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) attack involves multiple sources sending unwanted traffic towards a target.

The main security principle affected is Availability.

6.5 Insider Threats

An insider threat can come from someone who already has legitimate access to an organisation's systems.

It can be:

Malicious: Someone deliberately misuses access.

Negligent: Someone carelessly exposes information.

Compromised: An employee's account is taken over by an unauthorised person.

Organisations use access controls, activity monitoring and security awareness training to reduce insider risks.

6.6 Password Attacks

These are attempts to gain unauthorised access by compromising account credentials.

Common terms:

Brute force: Repeatedly attempting different password combinations.

Dictionary attack: Attempting passwords from a prepared list of common words or passwords.

Credential stuffing: Reusing credentials exposed in one breach to attempt access to other services.

Password spraying: Trying a small number of common passwords across multiple accounts.

Defensive controls include multifactor authentication, strong unique passwords, login monitoring and account lockout or rate-limiting policies.

7. Security Analyst Scenario: A Phishing Incident

A company's employee receives an email claiming to be from the company's IT department. The email says the employee must verify their account immediately.

The employee follows the instructions and enters their login credentials on a fake website.

An unauthorised person subsequently accesses the employee's account.

Analysis

Question

Answer

What is the threat actor?

The person behind the phishing attempt

What is the attack vector?

Email

What technique was used?

Phishing and social engineering

What information was exposed?

Login credentials

What could be affected?

Confidentiality and possibly integrity and availability

What could help prevent it?

MFA, email filtering and security awareness

This is how a security analyst starts thinking: identify what happened, understand how it happened, assess the impact and recommend controls.

11. Key Takeaways

This lecture introduced important cybersecurity concepts:

Threat actors are the people or groups behind potentially harmful activities.

Threat actors have different motivations and capabilities.

Attack vectors are the paths used to attempt compromise.

Malware, phishing, social engineering, DoS and password attacks are common cybersecurity threats.

Security analysts identify threats, understand their impact and recommend defensive controls.
